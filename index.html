Executive Summary (What this market is doing to Cook)

Bloomington is a tight-labor, institution-heavy market (IU + healthcare + life sciences) where hourly manufacturing roles face competition pressure and housing affordability friction. Cook’s advantage is brand presence and an established ecosystem; the risk is vacancy cost and churn in a low-unemployment environment.

  • Primary recruiting constraint: labor tightness (low unemployment) increases time-to-fill and raises the value of retention.
  • Primary retention constraint: housing + wage competition—especially for minimum-skills workers.
  • Where Cook can win: schedule stability, reliable hours, internal mobility ladders, and faster hiring cycle time.

Market Diagnosis (Labor Supply & Demand)

  • Labor supply: Monroe County has high educational attainment, creating strong potential for upskilling pipelines (HS+ and BA+ are both high).
  • Demand intensity: large institutional employers and life-science manufacturers compete for reliable attendance and shift-fit talent.
  • Hiring reality: in a 2–4% unemployment environment, “posting a job” isn’t a strategy—speed, offer quality, and onboarding experience matter.

Working assumption (until Cook confirms): we treat Cook’s target as a “minimum-skills hourly manufacturing basket” (assemblers, packers, machine operators, material movers, forklift, entry QA/inspection). Once Cook shares exact titles, we will map them to this basket and tighten the benchmarks.

PESTLE Diagnosis (Non-Market Conditions)

Political / Legal

  • Right-to-Work environment shapes labor relations expectations.
  • Watch: state/local incentives that accelerate competitor expansions (labor demand shocks).

Economic

  • Low unemployment increases vacancy cost and makes retention ROI unusually high.
  • Housing costs act as a “hidden wage”: if rent rises, hourly wage pressure follows.

Social / Demographic

  • Student + institutional population can create seasonality in entry-level labor.
  • Watch: workforce participation rate and “stable workforce” availability (non-student households).

Technological

  • Opportunity: partner with local training providers for entry-level certifications and fast-track onboarding.
  • Watch: competitor investments in automation that change wage expectations for operators/techs.

Environmental

  • Commute disruption risk (seasonal weather) can affect attendance and overtime reliability.
  • Watch: labor shed resilience (how far workers can reliably commute).

Institutional / Ecosystem

  • Large anchors (IU + healthcare) shape benefit expectations and talent movement.
  • Watch: life sciences growth that tightens the hourly market and increases poaching.

Bloomington Watchlist KPIs (What Cook should monitor monthly/quarterly)

  • Unemployment rate (MSA): early indicator of tightening/loosening labor.
  • Local production/warehouse wage drift (P50 & P75 once role-level data is added): pay competitiveness + compression risk.
  • Competitor job posting velocity (90-day rolling): hiring surges and poaching risk.
  • Housing affordability trend (rent + home values): retention risk for hourly workers.
  • Labor force participation: “true supply” beyond unemployment.

Shift note: Cook hasn’t confirmed 1st vs 2nd/3rd shift targeting yet. When confirmed, we’ll add a “shift differential pressure” section (night/weekend pay patterns + competitor schedules).
